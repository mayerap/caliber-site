What to run after your program ends

When a beginner program stops working, move to one with planned blocks: a phase of higher volume, a phase of heavier weights, then a lighter week. Retest your maxes first, decide your next program before the current one ends, and give the new one a full cycle.

Signs you have outgrown your program

Beginner programs add weight every session or every week. That works for months, then it stops. You have probably reached the end when:

  • You have reset the same lift two or three times and keep stalling at about the same weight.
  • Your AMRAP sets keep shrinking even though you are sleeping and eating well.
  • Every session feels like a test, and the warm-ups feel heavy.
  • The program’s own stall rules (like GZCLP’s stages) no longer produce progress.

None of this means you did something wrong. It means you are strong enough that adding weight every week is no longer realistic. The fix is a change in structure, not more effort.

Why blocks work after linear progression

Intermediate lifters progress over weeks, not sessions. Block periodization organizes training into phases, each with one job. It is a well-established approach in strength coaching; Issurin’s 2010 review in Sports Medicine is a good overview. A simple version:

  1. Build: more sets at moderate weights. This adds muscle and work capacity.
  2. Push: fewer sets at heavier weights. This turns that capacity into strength on the main lifts.
  3. Recover: a lighter week. Fatigue clears so you can show the strength you built.

Then you start the next block from your new numbers. Each block is a small, clear step, and you can see the trend over months.

A sample first block

Here is what a simple six-week block can look like for one main lift, using effort targets instead of fixed percentages. Pick weights that hit the target RPE, then let the effort guide small changes week to week.

WeekPhaseMain liftTarget effort
1Build4 sets of 8RPE 7 (3 reps left)
2Build4 sets of 8RPE 7.5
3Build5 sets of 6RPE 8 (2 reps left)
4Push4 sets of 4RPE 8
5Push3 sets of 3RPE 8.5 to 9
6Recover2 sets of 5RPE 6, then retest at the end of the week

Accessories follow the same rhythm: more sets early in the block, fewer in the push weeks, and about half the usual work in the lighter week. If RPE is new to you, read that guide first.

Retest, then reset your weights

Between programs, take a lighter week and then work up to a heavy set of 3 to 5 reps on each main lift. Estimate your max from it with a standard formula. A common one is Epley: estimated max = weight × (1 + reps ÷ 30). Use those numbers, not your old ones, to set the next program’s weights.

Be honest with the retest. Count only reps with form you would be happy to repeat. An inflated max just means stalling sooner in the next program.

Why program hopping stalls people

The gap between programs is where many lifters lose months. They finish a program, feel unsure, try something new for three weeks, read about something else, and switch again. Every switch resets the learning curve and hides the trend in their numbers.

The fix is to decide what comes next before the current program ends. Know which program you will run, when you will retest, and what a lighter week looks like. Then the transition takes one week, not one month.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need a deload between programs?

A lighter week is a good idea. Cut your sets roughly in half and keep the weights moderate for a week. Fatigue clears, and the strength you built shows up when you retest.

How do I retest my maxes?

You don’t need a true one-rep max. Work up to a heavy set of 3 to 5 with good form and estimate your max from it. That is safer, and it is accurate enough to set the next program’s weights.

Can I just rerun the same program?

Sometimes. If you finished a program that has planned phases and you are still progressing, running it again from new maxes can work. If you stalled on a linear program, rerunning it usually stalls again in the same place.

How long should a training block be?

Four to eight weeks is common. Caliber uses six-week blocks: volume weeks first, heavier weeks next, then a lighter week.

Is program hopping really that bad?

Changing programs is fine. Changing every few weeks is the problem, because no program gets time to work and you never see a clear trend in your numbers.
